Does low attrition mean a team is healthy?

I invented a company of 800 engineers, put it through three rounds of layoffs, and watched who quietly stopped trying. Teams that were easy to leave and teams that were not slid at almost the same pace. What mattered was how safe people felt speaking up, and how afraid they were of AI.

The headline that started this

On Tuesday, March 31, 2026, Oracle began emailing employees around 6 a.m. to say it was their last day. Estimates of the cut ran from 20,000 to 30,000 jobs, and CNBC later settled on 21,000. SkillSyncer's tracker ranks it the largest layoff of 2026.

Now think about who is still at their desk the next morning. They have watched a much bigger wave build (the same tracker counted 519 layoffs and 225,122 workers across all industries by September 24) and they are quietly pricing their own options. If those options look thin, they do not resign. A manager sees no letter and assumes things are fine.

That assumption is what I wanted to test. I am saying nothing about Oracle's people. Aegis is invented, and so is everyone in it.

Sources: Forbes, Computerworld, and the SkillSyncer Layoffs Tracker (CC BY 4.0).

What the theory predicted, and what the data said

Before simulating anything, I wrote down what organizational psychology would expect. Then I checked each prediction against the model.

Hirschman: when you cannot leave and cannot speak, you go quiet
Albert Hirschman, later extended by Caryl Rusbult, described four responses to a bad situation: exit, voice, loyalty and neglect. The speaking up half held up, as the next entry shows. The cannot leave half did not show as faster disengagement, because I wrote mobility to decide who exits, not who disengages.
Edmondson: people stay engaged where concerns get heard
Amy Edmondson's psychological safety was the strongest protective factor, cutting the risk to roughly a third for each extra point.
Brockner and Hobfoll: threat to security drains effort
Joel Brockner's work on layoff survivors and Stevan Hobfoll's Conservation of Resources both expect insecurity to pull effort back. AI displacement anxiety was the strongest risk factor, nearly tripling the risk per point, and downsizing exposure added more on top.
Bakker and Demerouti: demands without resources wear people down
Only half tested. The resources side shows up as safety, but I did not model workload, so I cannot say whether it mattered.
A word on quiet quitting. I use it to mean staying while doing the minimum, which is disengagement in the sense William Kahn and Wilmar Schaufeli describe. The dataset flags it when a hidden score passes a cutoff I picked, so treat it as a modeling choice and not a measured fact.

Layoff waves land at months 9, 19 and 28, the dotted lines in the chart at the top. Each one leaves a team more downsized, its people more anxious and less sure it is safe to speak. Between waves everyone drifts a little, and about one engineer in fifty goes through a reorg in any given month.

At any point an engineer is in one of three places:

  • Still engaged. Where everyone starts.
  • Quiet quitting. Their hidden score has crossed the cutoff. Once flagged, always flagged.
  • Gone. They resigned and leave the data.

The twist I wrote in: disengaged engineers on the high mobility team, ML Platform, tend to leave. Disengaged engineers everywhere else tend to stay. That single rule is why an exit rate and an engagement level can tell opposite stories.

Why not just predict who quits?

An ordinary prediction model asks a yes or no question at one moment. This question needs a when, and it has to cope with people I lose track of. Survival analysis handles both. Someone who resigns, or who is still engaged when I stop watching, is censored: they tell me something up to a point and then go silent.

Two tools from lifelines

lifelines is the Python library behind both. The Kaplan Meier estimator draws the curve at the top of this page. The Cox proportional hazards model asks how much each factor tilts that curve: a hazard ratio of 2 means double the risk, and 0.5 means half. Its concordance score is 0.79, meaning that for two engineers it ranks the earlier crosser higher about 79 times in 100.

Two factors do most of the work. Each extra point of psychological safety cuts the risk to about a third, and each extra point of AI anxiety multiplies it by nearly three. Downsizing exposure counts as well, with a catch described under the first caveat below. The three outlined bars (mobility, reorgs, tenure) cannot be told apart from no effect at all.

So, back to the opening question. In this simulation, how hard a team was to leave told me nothing about how fast it disengaged, because I built it that way. What I take from that: an exit rate measures who is able to leave. Engagement needs its own measurement.

Downsizing and tenure are converted from the model's raw units (per 100 points, per month) into units a person can picture.

Read this before quoting a number

  • I wrote the answer into the data. Anxiety, safety and downsizing drive disengagement in my generator, so the model finding them proves the plumbing works and nothing more.
  • The downsizing number is probably too big. I measured exposure across each person's whole window, which includes months after they had already disengaged. A model with time varying inputs would fix that.
  • 47.5% is an estimate, not a head count. 311 of 800 (38.9%) actually crossed. The gap exists because people who resigned early are treated as censored.
  • The sliders predict, they do not prove. They apply the fitted ratios to the overall curve.
  • The survey scores are invented. Anxiety and safety come from a generator on a 1 to 5 scale, not from a validated questionnaire.
